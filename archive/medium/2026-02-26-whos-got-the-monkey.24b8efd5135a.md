In 1974, William Oncken Jr. and Donald L. Wass published a short article in the Harvard Business Review called _“Management Time: Who’s Got the Monkey?”_ It went on to become one of the two best-selling HBR reprints of all time. I often mention this paper to my (more junior) colleagues but they are discouraged by the seven (!) page [PDF](https://www.google.com/search?q=who%27s+got+the+monkey+pdf) ;) So here is the short(er) version, Gen-Z-proof.

The paper is still very relevant today because the problem it describes has not gone away. If anything, in a world of Slack messages, constant stand-ups, and always-on collaboration, the monkey has more places to jump than ever before.

Here is what the paper actually says, distilled into its core ideas.

## The Central Metaphor: The Monkey

A “monkey” is the next action on a problem. It is not the problem itself — it is _who has to make the next move_. The entire article revolves around one observation: in most manager–subordinate interactions, the next move quietly shifts from the subordinate to the manager, without either party fully noticing.

**What this looks like in practice:** A teammate catches you after standup and says, “Hey, we have a problem with the deployment pipeline — I’m not sure what to do.” You reply, “Let me think about it and get back to you.” Congratulations: the monkey just jumped from their back to yours. They walk away free. You walk away with another task on your list. And later, _they_ will be the one checking in on _you_ — effectively supervising their own manager.

## The Three Types of Time

Oncken and Wass break a manager’s time into three buckets:

*   **Boss-imposed time** is the work your own manager requires of you. You cannot ignore it without consequences.
*   **System-imposed time** covers cross-functional obligations — peer requests, process compliance, organisational coordination. Ignoring these also carries penalties, though they tend to be slower and less direct.
*   **Self-imposed time** is everything else: things you initiate or agree to do. This is the only bucket you actually control, and it splits into two parts — time consumed by your subordinates’ problems (subordinate-imposed time) and the time left over for your own priorities (discretionary time).

The key insight: your leverage as a manager lives in discretionary time. Every monkey you accept from a report shrinks that pool. Accept enough of them, and you have zero time left for the strategic work that only you can do — which, ironically, is why you were promoted in the first place.

## How Monkeys Jump: Four Patterns to Recognise

The paper walks through four scenarios that are instantly recognisable in any modern workplace:

*   **The hallway handoff.** Someone raises a problem in passing. You say, “Let me think about it.” The monkey is now yours. They are waiting; you are working.
*   **The memo trap.** You ask someone to write up their thinking. They do. Now the document is in your inbox, and the next move is yours. The longer you sit on it, the more they spin their wheels.
*   **The open-ended offer.** You tell someone, “Just let me know how I can help.” They cannot move until you review their work, but your review queue is perpetually full. The monkey is effectively yours by default.
*   **The draft-first mistake.** A new initiative needs objectives. You say, “I will draft something for us to discuss.” You now own the next move on _their_ project. They are blocked until you deliver.

In every case, the transfer is subtle. Both parties walk away believing they are being collaborative. But the operational reality is that the subordinate is now idle and the manager is now busier.

## The Fix: Five Degrees of Initiative

Oncken and Wass propose a simple ladder of initiative that any team member can operate on:

1.   At the lowest level, a person **waits until told** what to do.
2.   One step up, they **ask what to do**.
3.   Then comes **recommending an action and executing once approved**.
4.   Higher still, they **act first and inform immediately after**.
5.   At the highest level, they **act independently and report routinely**.

The paper’s prescription is blunt: levels 1 and 2 should be off-limits for any professional. If you only wait or ask, you surrender all control over both the timing and the content of your work. The goal is to operate at level 3 or above — come with a recommendation, not just a problem.

For managers, the job is twofold: eliminate levels 1 and 2 from your team’s vocabulary, and for every problem that leaves your office, agree explicitly on what level of initiative applies and when the next check-in happens.

## The Five Rules for “Care and Feeding of Monkeys”

The paper closes with five rules designed to keep monkeys on the right backs:

**Rule 1 — Feed them or shoot them.** Every open item either gets active attention or gets killed. Letting monkeys starve wastes time on post-mortems and resurrections. 

**Rule 2 — Cap the population.** You can only sustain a finite number of active items. Your reports will work on as many problems as you have capacity to support — no more. 

**Rule 3 — Feed by appointment only.** Do not chase down status updates ad hoc. Scheduled check-ins keep both sides disciplined. 

**Rule 4 — Feed face-to-face or by phone, never by email.** Asynchronous communication hands the next move back to the manager. Conversation keeps the monkey on the subordinate’s back. 

**Rule 5 — Every monkey needs a next feeding time and an agreed level of initiative.** No ambiguity. No “let’s circle back sometime.” A specific date and a specific degree of ownership.

## The Covey Update: Empowerment Is Harder Than It Sounds

When HBR republished the article in 1999, Stephen Covey added a commentary acknowledging that the original advice — “give the monkey back” — was necessary but not sufficient. You cannot simply refuse to take on a subordinate’s problem if they lack the skill or confidence to solve it themselves. Effective delegation requires investing in people, building trust, and creating a culture where initiative is rewarded rather than punished.

Covey also noted a subtler dynamic: many managers are _eager_ to collect monkeys because holding problems feels like holding power. Letting go requires what he called a “mentality of abundance” — the belief that a subordinate’s growth does not come at your expense.
